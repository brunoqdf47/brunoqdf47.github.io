01

The core idea

Before any tool, one insight shapes everything — and it's the thing most automation initiatives miss.

You don't structure the department first and automate second. The way requests enter and are governed is the department.

An automation hub is not a pile of bots. It's an operating model: how work comes in, how it's qualified, prioritised, delivered, measured and sustained. Build that intake-and-governance system well, and the "department" exists. Skip it, and you have a talented person doing favours until they leave. So I treat the two as one job — and the stages below are how I do it.

The method — six stages

A DMAIC backbone, adapted. Tap any stage to expand the practices, the artifacts it produces, and the tools behind it.

What I do
  • Write a one-page charter — scope, boundaries, what success means.
  • Map suppliers, inputs, outputs and customers (SIPOC) so "is this even our request?" stops being a debate.
  • Map stakeholders across the business and tech sides before committing to anything.
  • Choose the methodology deliberately — full DMAIC here, but the choice itself is a decision, not a reflex.

From experienceMatching rigour to context is where time in the field pays off. Not every initiative needs the full apparatus — over-engineering the process is its own failure mode. Knowing when to apply less is part of what experience teaches.
What I do
  • Stand up one standardised request form, ID-stamped per submission, so every idea is traceable from first contact.
  • Run a Kanban board (Planner / boards) with an SLA defined per stage — intake, analysis, build, validation — so nothing stalls invisibly.
  • Turn each request into a tracked ticket: a single method for generating, prioritising and managing automation demand.
  • Keep a transparent backlog leadership can read at a glance.

Why this step mattersThe form is where business pain gets translated into a tech-actionable spec — a request written by a plant manager becomes something a developer can build. Most initiatives underinvest here; that two-way translation is quietly the whole job.
What I do
  • Map the AS-IS process end to end, then validate it with a structured interview — never trusting a single verbal account.
  • Assess process discovery: is there process-mining or task-mining tooling available? If yes, use the data. If not, proceed with structured manual discovery — the realistic path in most early-stage environments.
  • Capture a baseline before automation starts, so value can be proven later, not just estimated.
  • Run a focused gap analysis to find root causes, not symptoms.

What I do
  • Pick the right tool for the job, not the favourite tool: RPA (UiPath) for legacy and SAP-UI work, Power Automate for M365-native flows, Make.com / n8n for API orchestration.
  • Bring AI in where it earns its place — LLM integration (e.g. the Anthropic API) for the unstructured work that classic automation can't touch: reading documents, classifying, drafting, screening.
  • Build against a standard design doc (PDD / SDD) so the automation is maintainable, not a black box.
  • Test against the baseline before calling it done.

What I do
  • Track KPI and ROI against the captured baseline — realised value, not the number promised at intake.
  • Run an automation feedback form after delivery, so the user's real experience feeds the next iteration.
  • Revisit projects already in flight and bring them into the same standard, retroactively.
  • Set a 30 / 60 / 90-day follow-up so the feedback loop actually closes.

What I do

03

What makes the method hold

A method like this runs on something harder to copy than a tool stack: someone standing in the gap between business and technology, fluently, in both directions. It's worth naming what that capability actually is.

Two-way translation

Business ↔ technology, both directions

A business pain becomes a technical spec; a technical constraint becomes language leadership can act on. Most initiatives fail somewhere in that translation — so the method treats it as a first-class skill, not an afterthought.

Business need ⇄ Tech solution
A note on profiles

BAs with roots in IT, RPA & AI

The typical business analyst comes from business or admin and adds technology thinly on top. The principle underneath

The automation is the easy part. The system around it — intake, governance, measurement, documentation — is what makes the value last past the demo, past the hype, and past any one person. That's where the real work is.

Shared from fifteen-plus years across multinational environments — roots in IT and automation, the analysis layer built on top — in case it's useful to anyone standing up AI and automation as a repeatable system rather than a series of one-off wins.
